## A02 — Why Google Was Created / AI Patents

**What challenged me this module:**
Connecting two things that don't obviously belong in the same report: why
Google was founded in 1998, and how many GenAI patents got filed between
2014-2023. The link is that both are stories about a founding technical
insight (PageRank; the Transformer) triggering an entire industry's worth
of downstream filings.

**What clicked that didn't before:**
The patent data isn't smooth growth — it's four sharp step-changes tied to
specific dated events (2017 Transformer, 2020 GPT-3, 2022 ChatGPT, 2023
record year). Innovation in this field doesn't accumulate gradually; it
jumps every time a research paper becomes a usable product.

**How I'd explain this concept to someone who knows nothing about AI:**
Google won because it ranked pages by who linked to them, not by how many
times a keyword was stuffed in. Twenty years later, the same pattern
repeated with AI patents — once "Attention Is All You Need" (2017) showed
a genuinely better architecture, patent filings didn't just grow, they
exploded.

**One thing I want to go deeper on:**
Whether patent volume is actually a good proxy for real innovation, or
just a proxy for how many companies feel legally exposed enough to file
defensively once a technology looks valuable.

**How this connects to the broader AI landscape:**
Over 25% of all GenAI patents ever filed came out in 2023 alone — that's
not steady progress, that's a land grab. Worth remembering next time "AI is
moving fast" gets said as a vague feeling instead of a measurable fact.

## L02 — Key People & Foundational Work in AI History

**What challenged me this module:**
Tracing each of the 11 people's work all the way to a system in use today,
not just listing their historical contribution. It's one thing to say
"Rosenblatt built the Perceptron in 1958," another to trace the direct line
from that hardware to every deep learning layer running right now.

**What clicked that didn't before:**
Nearly every row's funding source is military, government, or corporate
R&D — DARPA, ONR, NSF, Bell Labs, IBM. Foundational AI research was never
neutral academic curiosity running in a vacuum; it was funded because
someone with power wanted the capability.

**How I'd explain this concept to someone who knows nothing about AI:**
Claude Shannon's 1948 paper on information theory defined what a "bit" is.
That's not ancient trivia — every file you download, every message you
send, still runs on his math. The people in this spreadsheet aren't
history, they're infrastructure.

**One thing I want to go deeper on:**
How Minsky's 1969 Perceptrons critique — which set back neural network
research for over a decade — actually got overturned once Hinton's 1986
backpropagation paper showed multi-layer networks could work. What
specifically changed the field's mind, beyond "the math got better"?

**How this connects to the broader AI landscape:**
Claude Shannon's row is flagged in the spreadsheet as "★ namesake of Claude
AI" — a small detail, but a real reminder that the tools I use every day in
this course are named after, and built on top of, exactly the lineage this
spreadsheet documents.

## Puzzle 02 — Google's Hat Puzzle

**What challenged me this module:**
My first instinct was that the hat puzzle was unsolvable — you can't know
what you can't see. That assumption itself was the obstacle: I was trying
to solve it person-by-person instead of looking at the group as a system.

**What clicked that didn't before:**
Person 1 doesn't need to save themselves — they need to give everyone else
what they need to save themselves. Once I saw the parity rule as "one light
switch, on or off," the whole 100-person strategy became obvious instead of
abstract.

**How I'd explain this concept to someone who knows nothing about AI:**
One person agrees ahead of time to be the signal for everyone else. They
call out whether they see an odd or even number of red hats. Everyone
behind them can now do the math and know their own hat color for certain —
even though they never saw it. One shared rule turns 100 guesses into 99
guarantees.

**One thing I want to go deeper on:**
How multi-agent reinforcement learning actually implements this kind of
minimal-signal coordination in practice, beyond the conceptual mapping.

**How this connects to the broader AI landscape:**
The rule itself is the intelligence — not any individual prisoner. That's
the same as an AI model: it doesn't have complete information about the
world, it operates on learned patterns and shared frameworks. There's also
a personal lesson in here — I've had times where I wouldn't move until I
had everything figured out completely, and this puzzle is proof that
optimal decisions don't require complete information. They require the
right framework and the discipline to apply it consistently.
