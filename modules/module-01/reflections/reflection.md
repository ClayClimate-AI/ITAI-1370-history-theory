# Reflection — Module 01

**What challenged me this module:**
Compressing 70+ years of AI history into a paper that actually argues
something, instead of just listing dates. The hard part wasn't finding facts
— it was picking the one thread (symbolic rules vs. learned patterns) and
making every section serve it.

**What clicked that didn't before:**
The "AI winters" pattern isn't a side note — it's the whole shape of the
field's history repeating: overpromise, funding collapse, a sidelined idea
surviving anyway. Neural networks got dismissed after Minsky's 1969
Perceptron critique and didn't come back as the mainstream approach until
decades later. That's a useful lesson outside AI too: being wrong about
timing isn't the same as being wrong about the idea.

**How I'd explain this concept to someone who knows nothing about AI:**
Every time a machine gets good at something, people redefine "real
intelligence" as whatever it still can't do. Beating a chess champion used
to be the proof. Now it's a phone app. The finish line keeps moving because
we keep redefining it after the machine crosses it.

**One thing I want to go deeper on:**
How interpretability research actually works — right now "the model is a
black box" is a fact I can state but not really explain mechanically.

**How this connects to the broader AI landscape:**
The bias research (Buolamwini & Gebru) isn't a historical footnote — it's the
same failure mode showing up in every system that learns from real-world
data, including anything I'd build professionally. Understanding *why* it
happens (biased training data → biased output, dressed up as objective math)
is what separates using AI responsibly from just trusting the output because
it sounds confident.
